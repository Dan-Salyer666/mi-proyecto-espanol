Frases, marcos y patrones de uso

El español construye con tener decenas de estados que el inglés expresa con to be. No son excepciones sueltas: son un mismo patrón. El sustantivo que elijas decide si hay artículo, qué cuantificador concuerda, qué preposición sigue, si el imperativo está disponible y si el pretérito cambia el sentido. La lista es el contenido; la gramática es la página.

NOTA*

English speakers hit two problems with this construction, and both come from translating to be + adjective instead of learning the pattern.

There is no article

Tengo hambre, not tengo una hambre or tengo la hambre. The noun sits bare, and this is the default across the whole family — sed, sueño, frío, calor, miedo, razón, prisa. The exceptions are few enough to name outright:

  • tener la culpa
  • tener la intención de
  • tener la costumbre de

Learn those three as marked; assume bare everywhere else.

The intensifier is mucho, not muy

This follows automatically once you accept that hambre is a noun, not an adjective — muy modifies adjectives and adverbs, mucho modifies nouns. So mucha hambre, mucho miedo, mucha prisa. The word agrees in gender and number with the noun, and that agreement has to become automatic rather than calculated.

The hambre trap

Hambre is feminine, but it takes el in the singular — el hambre — because Spanish swaps la for el before a stressed a- sound to avoid the collision (the same reason for el agua, el águila). The gender itself never changes, so every agreeing word stays feminine: mucha hambre, tanta hambre. Getting el hambre right and then saying mucho hambre is the classic overcorrection.

On age

Tengo cuarenta años, never soy cuarenta. Spanish treats years as something you possess, and this is one of the highest-frequency instances of the whole construction.

NOTA*

Two things live here, and the second is the more consequential.

The a / de split with miedo

The usable generalization: a points at the thing feared, de points at an outcome or a clause. Le tengo miedo a los perros — dogs are the target. Tengo miedo de caerme — falling is the feared outcome. Tengo miedo de que se entere — a clause follows. Both prepositions are heard with a plain noun, and tengo miedo de los perros is not wrong, but the a version is the more natural default in Latin America when the fear has an object. Where a clause or an infinitive follows, de is effectively obligatory — that is the part worth locking down.

The dative you are leaving out

A whole subgroup — miedo, cariño, manía, envidia, celos, bronca, respeto, pánico — takes a redundant indirect object pronoun when the complement is a person or a specific thing: Le tengo miedo a ese perro, not just Tengo miedo a ese perro. English has no equivalent, so English speakers systematically omit it, and the result is grammatical but immediately audible as foreign. The pronoun agrees with the complement, not the subject: Les tengo cariño a tus hermanos.

With envidia in particular, the dative construction tenerle envidia a alguien is the Latin American norm — reach for it before tener envidia de.

A useful asymmetry

The dative appears when the complement is specific. Le tengo miedo a ese perro (that dog) alongside Tengo miedo de los perros (dogs in general). Keep this in mind — it returns in section ③, where it turns out to govern the imperative too.

Two splits worth memorizing

  • interés en for fields and subjects (interés en la ciencia) · interés por for people and causes (interés por los pacientes) ?por confirmar
  • respeto a for authority or fear (tenerle respeto al mar) · respeto por for admiration (respeto por sus mayores) ?por confirmar

NOTA*

This is the rule a flat vocabulary list cannot give you, and getting it wrong produces sentences that are not merely odd but incoherent.

The test: can the person comply?

Ten cuidado works because carefulness is a stance you adopt — hearing the command is enough to produce the state. Ten hambre fails because no amount of willingness makes you hungry. The imperative presupposes that the addressee can bring about what is ordered, so the construction is available exactly when the noun names something volitional.

Three separate reasons for the block

  • Involuntary sensations (hambre, sed, sueño, frío, fiebre) — physically not under control.
  • Truth-conditions (razón, sentido, la culpa) — these are not states of a person at all. Tener razón describes whether what you said matches the facts; ordering someone to be right is a category error, not a rudeness.
  • Outcomes (éxito, suerte) — under nobody's control, so they cannot be ordered. But they can be wished for, which is where they resurface.

Where the blocked ones go: que + subjunctive

You cannot say ten éxito, but que tengas éxito is idiomatic and extremely frequent. The pattern generalizes — que tengas un buen día, que tengan buen viaje. The subjunctive is doing exactly what it does everywhere else: marking something as desired rather than asserted.

¡Suerte! on its own and que tengas suerte are the standard forms here. ¡Ten suerte! breaks the rule and is best avoided.

NOTA*

The affirmative/negative asymmetry looks like a quirk and is actually the rule confirming itself.

Ten miedo is odd; no tengas miedo is one of the most common things anyone says in Spanish. The reason: you can be told to stop feeling something, but not to start. A negative imperative here is not ordering the absence of an emotion — it is asking the hearer to stand down. That much is within someone's control, so the construction is licensed. The same applies to no tengas vergüenza, no tengas prisa, no tengas miedo de preguntar.

The limit is exactly where you would predict

No tengas hambre is as impossible as ten hambre, because de-escalating hunger is not something a person can do either. So the asymmetry appears only with emotions — states you can partly talk yourself out of — and not with sensations. That split is the sharpest evidence that controllability, not politeness or convention, governs this card.

The affirmative survives when the fear has a target

  • Ten miedo. — odd, close to meaningless. An order to generate an emotion from nothing.
  • Tenle miedo a ese perro, que muerde. — perfectly natural.

What changed is not who is being commanded. Both are imperatives aimed at the listener, and in the second the clitic le is a dative agreeing with ese perro — it marks what the fear is directed at, not who receives the order. What changed is that the fear now has a specific, identified object, and that converts the request from "summon an emotion" into "adopt a wary stance toward this known danger." A stance toward a named thing is something a person can actually decide to take, so the imperative is licensed again.

This is the same specificity effect that governs the dative in section ②: le tengo miedo a ese perro (specific) alongside tengo miedo de los perros (general). The abstract version resists both the dative and the imperative; the targeted version accepts both. Where the affirmative imperative of an emotion works at all, it works because a target is present.

A second route past the block: the moral demand

¡Ten vergüenza! is also said, and it is not asking for an emotion either. It demands decency in the face of a shameless act — closer to "have some shame" as a reproach than to "feel ashamed." Once again the imperative survives because what is being ordered is a stance, not a sensation. ?por confirmar

Pretérito e imperfectoel estado y su inicio
Aspect

Con tener, la elección entre pretérito e imperfecto no es estilística. El imperfecto presenta el estado como fondo; el pretérito lo presenta como algo que empezó, o que ocupó un tramo cerrado de tiempo.

Imperfecto — fondoPretérito — inicio o tramo cerrado
Tenía hambre, así que me preparé algo.
I was hungry, so I made myself something.		 De repente tuve hambre.
I suddenly got hungry.
De niño tenía miedo a la oscuridad.
As a child I was afraid of the dark.		 Cuando se apagó la luz, tuve miedo.
When the light went out, I got scared.
Tuve que vs. tenía que
Tuve que salir temprano.
I had to leave early — and I did.
Tenía que salir temprano, pero al final me quedé.
I was supposed to leave early, but in the end I stayed.
Tuvimos que cancelar el viaje.
We had to cancel the trip — it happened.
El pretérito como obtener
Tuve una idea.
I had an idea. / An idea came to me.
Tuvimos noticias suyas la semana pasada.
We heard from him last week.
Tuvo una hija en marzo.
She had a daughter in March.
NOTA*

With most verbs, choosing preterite or imperfect changes the framing. With tener, it can change the meaning outright.

Imperfect = the state · preterite = the onset

Tenía miedo describes a condition that was simply in place — background against which something else happened. Tuve miedo reads as I got scared: the emphasis lands on the moment the state began. English usually needs a different verb to make the distinction (was afraid vs. got scared), which is why the contrast is easy to miss when translating.

The preterite also covers a bounded stretch: Tuve fiebre toda la semana is perfectly good, and the adverbial toda la semana does not contradict the preterite — it defines the boundaries that license it. The imperfect is available too (tenía fiebre toda la semana), describing the condition as the persisting backdrop rather than a closed episode.

The one that actually matters: tuve que vs. tenía que

Tuve que irme carries a strong implication that the departure happened — the obligation was discharged. Tenía que irme reports only that the obligation existed, and leaves the outcome open, which is why it is the natural setup for …pero no lo hice. English "had to" is ambiguous between the two, so this is a distinction you must build rather than translate. It is the same split poder shows with pude / podía.

The preterite's second job

In the preterite, tener often stops meaning possess and starts meaning obtain, receive, or experience: tuve una idea, tuvimos noticias, tuvo un accidente, tuvo un hijo. This is not a separate verb — it is the same onset reading, applied to a thing rather than a state. You did not possess an idea over a bounded interval; you came to have one at a point.

NOTA*

Three things, in order of how often they will bite you.

Emotion and desire take the subjunctive

Tener miedo de que, tener ganas de que, tener la esperanza de que all describe an attitude toward a situation rather than asserting that the situation holds — so the verb in the clause goes into the subjunctive. Tengo miedo de que se entere, never se entera. This is not a rule about tener; these phrases are simply members of the general subjunctive-of-emotion pattern.

Certainty takes the indicative

Tener la certeza de que, tener la seguridad de que assert that something is the case, so the clause stays indicative. Same verb, opposite mood, and the noun is what decides.

The negation flip

Tengo claro que asserts, so it takes the indicative. Negate it and the assertion disappears, and the subjunctive appears: No tengo claro que sea buena idea. This flip is worth practising because it is productive far beyond tener — the same thing happens with creo que / no creo que, es evidente que / no es evidente que.

One frequency note

Tener vergüenza de que is grammatical but heavy. In practice me da vergüenza que… dominates overwhelmingly — see the tener vs. dar note in section ⑦.

Dos errores opuestos, ambos muy frecuentes en el habla de todo el mundo hispano. El queísmo quita una preposición necesaria; el dequeísmo añade una que no corresponde. Ninguno de los dos se acepta en la lengua escrita.

NOTA*

These are worth learning together, because they are mirror images and because you will hear both constantly from native speakers — which makes them hard to un-learn once absorbed by ear.

Queísmo drops a preposition the phrase requires

Tengo miedo de los perros takes de before a noun, so it keeps de before a clause: tengo miedo de que venga. Dropping it gives tengo miedo que venga — extremely common in speech, wrong in writing. The same applies to me alegro de que, estoy seguro de que, me di cuenta de que, tengo ganas de que.

Dequeísmo inserts one the phrase does not license

Pensar, creer, decir, resultar and tener entendido take a bare que — nothing precedes it. Pienso de que is the hypercorrection of someone who has been told about queísmo and overshot.

The test that resolves both

Turn the sentence into a question and see which form the question takes. If you would ask ¿De qué tienes miedo?, the clause needs de que. If you would ask ¿Qué piensas?, it takes bare que. The question preserves the preposition the verb actually governs, which is exactly the information you need.

One caution about register

Queísmo in particular is so widespread in casual speech that avoiding it can sound careful. That is fine — careful is the right target, and it costs nothing in comprehension. Aim for the standard form and recognize the other when you hear it.

Parte II · Las familias
Reading the frame chips

Every entry below carries its full grammatical frame as a strip of labelled chips. The chips appear on every entry, including where a whole family shares the same values — so a missing chip always means "not applicable", never "not checked".

PREP The preposition that follows the phrase: de a con en por para sobre que. ninguna means the phrase takes no preposition at all. A slash (a / de) means both are used, usually with a difference in meaning.
IMP Whether the phrase works as a command. ✓ yes — ten cuidado. ✕ no — the noun names something you cannot produce on request. ✕ · neg ✓ the affirmative is blocked but the negative is fine: you can be told to stop feeling something (no tengas miedo), not to start. → que tengas the command is impossible because the outcome is outside anyone's control, so the phrase survives as a wish instead — not ten suerte but que tengas suerte.
CUANT The intensifier, which agrees in gender and number because the word is a noun: mucho mucha muchos muchas. Never muy. ninguna means the phrase is not intensified this way.
ASP pret ≠ imp — preterite and imperfect carry different meanings here, not just different framing. Tenía miedo is a background state; tuve miedo is the moment it started. See section ④.
GRAM A syntactic requirement of the phrase.
le + a — takes a redundant indirect-object pronoun alongside the person: __Le__ tengo miedo a ese perro. English has no equivalent, so English speakers drop it and sound foreign.
+ obj. directo — needs a direct object inside the phrase: ten el pasaporte a mano, not ten a mano.
+ infinitivo — followed by an infinitive.
+ subj — opens a clause in the subjunctive.
ind · neg → subj — indicative normally, subjunctive when negated.
sujeto inanimado — the subject is a thing or an idea, not a person.
lleva artículo — keeps its article, against the bare-noun default.
usa adjetivo — intensified with an adjective, not a quantifier: una gripe fuerte.
casi siempre negativo · neg · interrog — restricted to those contexts in practice.
REG Register: coloquial formal muy formal. Regional marking is shown separately, as a badge beside the phrase.
? Por confirmar. Usually a place where the controllability rule predicts a form should work. Prediction is not attestation — treat these as provisional until a native speaker confirms them. Listed in full near the bottom of the page.

NOTA* Spanish splits heat three ways where English uses one adjective, and picking the wrong verb changes the subject of the sentence. Person → tener: Tengo calor — I feel hot. NOTA*

Several of these emotions can be built two ways, and the choice is not stylistic.

Tener = a state · dar = a trigger

Tener presents the emotion as a state the subject is in: Tengo vergüenza — I feel ashamed, as a condition. Dar presents it as something produced in you by a trigger, with the trigger as grammatical subject and you as the indirect object: Me da vergüenza — it embarrasses me. The trigger can be named — Me da vergüenza hablar en público, Me da miedo la oscuridad — which is exactly what tener cannot do as cleanly.

Frequency matters here

For several of these, the dar version is simply the more idiomatic default. Me da vergüenza is far more common than tengo vergüenza; me da pena likewise. With miedo the two are closer and both are frequent. With ganas, only tener works.

The practical takeaway

When there is a specific thing causing the feeling, reach for dar. When you are describing your own condition without naming a cause, tener is right. And note that dar puts the feeling in the third person — me dan miedo los perros, plural, agreeing with los perros, not with me.

NOTA* Tener manía a alguien is peninsular. In Latin America, and especially in the Río de la Plata, the equivalents are tenerle bronca a alguien and tenerle idea a alguien — both with the obligatory dative. All of these are textual regional notes; the audio on this page stays neutral throughout.

Look at the imperative chips down this card and a split appears inside "character" that the controllability rule alone would not predict.

Nouns naming a posture you adopt — cuidado, paciencia, calma, fe, valor, tacto, respeto — accept the imperative. NOTA* No tienes por qué ir is three words: preposition plus interrogative qué, carrying its accent. It is not porque (because), not porqué (the noun, "the reason"), and not por que. The phrase literally asks after a reason, which is why the accent survives inside a statement — it is an embedded question.
NOTA*

English collapses two different denials into "don't have to," and Spanish keeps them apart.

Obligation vs. justification

No tener que denies an obligation. No tienes que ir — nobody is requiring it; go if you like. No tener por qué denies a justification. No tienes por qué ir — there is no reason that would make going appropriate. The second is stronger and more argumentative: it does not merely release you from a duty, it rejects the premise that one existed.

The use pattern is as important as the meaning

Tener por qué appears almost exclusively in negative and interrogative contexts. You will hear no tengo por qué, ¿por qué tengo que…?, no tienes por qué disculparte constantly; the bare affirmative tengo por qué ir is not idiomatic. Treat the negation as part of the expression rather than as something you apply to it.

Register

No tengo por qué frequently carries indignation — it is the phrase for refusing an imposition. Useful, but not neutral, and worth deploying on purpose.

For tener que in the past — tuve que vs. tenía que — see section ④.

Guía Rápida

Four tests. Run a new tener phrase through these and you have its whole frame.

1. Article — is there one?

Default is bare: tengo hambre, tengo miedo, tengo razón. Only three common phrases carry an article: la culpa, la intención de, la costumbre de.

2. Quantifier — mucho, never muy

It is a noun, so it takes mucho and agrees in gender and number: mucha hambre, mucho miedo, muchas ganas. Watch el hambre — masculine article, feminine agreement.

3. Imperative — can the person comply?

If the noun names a posture you adopt, yes: ten cuidado, ten paciencia. If it names a sensation, a truth or an outcome, no. Blocked outcomes reappear as wishes: que tengas suerte. Emotions block the affirmative but allow the negative: no tengas miedo.

4. Aspect — state or onset?

Imperfect gives the state as background (tenía miedo); preterite gives the onset or a closed span (tuve miedo = I got scared). With obligation: tuve que implies it happened, tenía que leaves it open.
